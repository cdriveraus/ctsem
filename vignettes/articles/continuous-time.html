Most of what psychologists study changes all the time: mood, stress, fitness, skills, relationships. This article is about why it is worth modelling how such things change, and why ctsem writes those models in continuous time. It fits no models, and uses only a little R to compute what a model implies.

library(ggplot2)

Questions about change

Does ice cream make people happier? An experiment could give one group ice cream and another nothing, and ask both how happy they are some time later. That estimates one effect, at one time after one treatment, in one setting. To use it anywhere else we need to know more. When does happiness rise, and for how long? What else changes with it? Does the effect depend on the amount, the weather or the person? A model of how happiness changes over time answers these for any time after the treatment, and so carries further than the single estimate.

Time itself is rarely the interest. It stands for everything that happens as it passes: after the ice cream come digestion, changes in mood and behaviour, and other people’s responses to them. A model of change over time describes what tends to follow from a change when nothing else is done.

Effects that pass through other variables take time too. A change in exercise habits changes fitness, but not within a day. A model of how the change in habits carries through to fitness over time says when the effect should show, which a test at one chosen delay cannot.

Such a model can also forecast. It predicts a person’s future from their own past, and from how other, similar people developed. With random effects ctsem does both. Each person’s predictions start from the population and are refined by their own observations (ctPredict(), see vignette('ctsem')).

Discrete time models

A cross-lagged panel model, or a vector autoregression, describes change as steps from one occasion to the next. This diagram shows such a model for three variables over four occasions. A affects B at the next occasion, and B affects C:

With yearly data, the model says that a change in A takes two years to begin to affect C. The same model fitted to weekly data says two weeks. Every coefficient means something different at a different interval, so studies with different intervals cannot be compared directly, and a study with unequal intervals does not fit the model at all.

Change between the occasions

Most constructs exist, and act on each other, between the occasions on which they are measured as well. A change in exercise begins to affect fitness straight away, though the effect takes months to show. In a continuous time model, A drives B and B drives C at every moment. The drift matrix holds these direct effects, with the effect of each column on each row:

drift <- matrix(c(
  -1,  0,  0,
   2, -1,  0,
   0,  1, -1), 3, 3, byrow = TRUE,
  dimnames = list(c('A', 'B', 'C'), c('A', 'B', 'C')))
drift
#>    A  B  C
#> A -1  0  0
#> B  2 -1  0
#> C  0  1 -1

The diagonal makes each process return towards its equilibrium after a change. A has no direct effect on C. The effects across an interval of Δt\Delta t that a discrete time model would estimate are the matrix exponential of the drift matrix times the interval. For observations a year apart:

round(expm::expm(drift * 1), 3)
#>       A     B     C
#> A 0.368 0.000 0.000
#> B 0.736 0.368 0.000
#> C 0.368 0.368 0.368

A now predicts C a year later, though it has no direct effect on it, because B changes during the year and A drives that change. Random shocks spread the same way. Shocks that are independent at each moment become correlated over a year, because a shock to A moves B and C before the year is out. The correlations of the residuals across one year, for independent shocks of equal size:

diffusion <- diag(3)
# the stationary covariance solves drift %*% S + S %*% t(drift) + diffusion = 0
S <- matrix(solve(kronecker(diag(3), drift) + kronecker(drift, diag(3)),
  -c(diffusion)), 3, 3)
transition <- expm::expm(drift * 1)
residual <- S - transition %*% S %*% t(transition)
round(cov2cor(residual), 2)
#>      A    B    C
#> A 1.00 0.52 0.17
#> B 0.52 1.00 0.40
#> C 0.17 0.40 1.00

To represent this continuous model at a one year interval, a discrete time model needs every path from each earlier variable to each later one, and correlated residuals at each occasion:

A model with few direct effects between continuously interacting processes implies a discrete time model with many lagged effects, at any interval, and whether the processes are fast or slow.

The course of an effect

The discrete time effects at each interval are points on a curve. Here is the response of each process to a change of one unit in A at time 0:

times <- seq(0, 6, by = 0.05)
response <- t(sapply(times, function(t) expm::expm(drift * t)[, 'A']))
course <- data.frame(time = rep(times, 3), process = rep(colnames(drift),
  each = length(times)), response = c(response))
ggplot(course, aes(time, response, colour = process)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1) +
  labs(x = 'years since the change in A', y = 'change') + theme_bw()

A returns towards its equilibrium from the start. B’s response is largest after 1 year, and C’s after 2 years. C begins to change at once, slowly, since the change reaches it only through B. B stays raised for a while after its peak, and keeps moving C. The lagged effect of A on B or C in a discrete time model is one point on these curves, chosen by the interval of the study. For a fitted model, ctDiscretePars() plots these curves with their uncertainty.

What follows

  • A lagged coefficient mixes the direct effect with the indirect ones over the interval. The hypothesis that A does not affect C directly is about the drift matrix. A discrete time model at the observed interval cannot test it, since the lagged effect of A on C is not zero.
  • Fixing lagged paths to zero, or shrinking them towards zero with penalties or priors to find which effects exist, acts on what the direct effects imply after one interval, not on the direct effects.
  • A continuous time model states the direct effects, and computes the effects for the intervals in the data, equal or not. It does not remove the need for observations close enough together to see how the processes respond.

ctsem fits discrete time models too, which suit data at a fixed interval when the interest is in that interval only: see Discrete time models.
